## Mechanism

Bernstein and Lange (KN-LIT-7e2964) observed that the standard security
argument "rho costs `sqrt(n)`" is a *uniform* statement. A non-uniform
attacker who is allowed to depend on the group (which every real attacker
is, since the group is a fixed standard) precomputes a table of `S`
distinguished points from random walks of length about `T = sqrt(n/S)`. A
later instance `Q = kP` is solved by walking from `Q` until it hits a stored
distinguished point, in expected time `T`. Corrigan-Gibbs and Kogan
(KN-LIT-013) proved in the generic group model that `S * T^2 = Omega(n)`, so
this is optimal up to constants and no cleverer table exists.

## As a trapdoor

The "secret" is the table. A standardizer who publishes one curve and spends
`n^{2/3}` once holds a per-instance advantage of `n^{1/6}` over rho forever,
and nothing in the public parameters reveals the investment. It is the only
entry in the taxonomy (KN-TECH-6ead00) whose secret is *computational* rather
than *structural*: there is no hidden property of the curve at all.

## Detectability

None from the parameters. The only defence is size: at 256 bits the one-time
cost is about `2^170`, out of reach; at 160 bits (`2^107`) it is within the
reach of a large actor and the per-instance cost `2^53` is trivial. This
is one of the arguments for retiring 160-bit and 192-bit curves and for
per-application base points where multiple curves are cheap.

## Applicability limits

- Table sizes: `S = n^{1/3}` at 256 bits is `2^85` entries, itself infeasible
  to store; the balanced point is not the interesting one there. At smaller
  `n` both `S` and `T` are practical.
- Does not compose with the other entries into something stronger; it is an
  additive constant-exponent gain and cannot yield a subexponential attack.

## Relevance

The program's baseline convention (KN-TECH-006) charges `0.886 sqrt(n)` fully;
a claimed improvement must state whether it is uniform or relies on
precomputation, since `n^{1/3}` per instance is *already* achievable non-
uniformly and is therefore not a break. Any "trapdoor" claim on a fixed curve
must likewise be compared against this free baseline.
