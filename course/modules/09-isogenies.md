> separability, the kernel ↔ isogeny dictionary, Vélu's formulas, dual

## 1. Definition

An **isogeny** φ : E → E′ is a nonconstant map given by rational
functions that sends O to O. Two miracles come free:

* **it is automatically a group homomorphism** (Silverman III.4.8 —
  a map of curves preserving one point preserves the whole group
  structure);
* it is automatically **surjective** on F̄-points, with **finite
  kernel**.

Isogenies are "the right maps" in this world: strong enough to preserve
all structure, loose enough to connect *different* curves. When an
isogeny E → E′ exists, E and E′ are **isogenous** — an equivalence
relation (duals, §5, give symmetry).

First examples:

| isogeny | kernel | degree |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [m] : P ↦ mP, E → E | E[m] ≅ (ℤ/m)² | m² |
| Frobenius (x,y) ↦ (x^p, y^p), E → E^(p) | {O} | p |
| quotient E → E/⟨T⟩ (Vélu, §4) | ⟨T⟩ | ord(T) |

## 2. Degree and separability

Writing φ(x, y) = (u(x)/v(x), y·s(x)/t(x)) in lowest terms, the
**degree** of φ is max(deg u, deg v) — the generic number of preimages
of a point. Multiplicativity: deg(ψ∘φ) = deg ψ · deg φ.

φ is **separable** if (equivalently) it has deg φ distinct kernel
points; the derivative test u′v − uv′ ≢ 0 detects it. Then:

  **separable ⇒ #ker φ = deg φ.**

The prototypical *inseparable* isogeny is Frobenius: x ↦ x^p has
derivative p·x^(p−1) = 0 in characteristic p; degree p but trivial
kernel. Every isogeny factors as (separable) ∘ (Frobenius power). In
this course all isogenies of degree < p are automatically separable —
inseparability only enters through Frobenius, and "[p] purely
inseparable" was precisely supersingularity criterion 2 (module 08).

## 3. The fundamental dictionary

**Theorem.** Let Φ ⊆ E(F̄) be any finite subgroup. There is a curve
E/Φ and a separable isogeny φ : E → E/Φ with kernel exactly Φ, unique
up to post-composition with isomorphisms.

  **finite subgroups of E ⟷ separable isogenies out of E**

This is the quotient-group construction of module 02 §4 made geometric —
and it is why "choose a secret subgroup" = "choose a secret isogeny" in
every protocol of module 11. Composing along prime factorizations:
a cyclic kernel of order ℓ₁ℓ₂⋯ gives a chain of prime-degree steps —
walks in a graph, one ℓ-isogeny at a time.

## 4. Vélu's formulas (1971): the dictionary, executably

Input: E : y² = x³ + ax + b and a finite subgroup, say generated by T.
Output: explicit E′ and φ. For the kernel points Q = (x_Q, y_Q) ≠ O,
taking one representative per pair {Q, −Q} (2-torsion points count once):

  g_Q = 3x_Q² + a, u_Q = (2y_Q)², t_Q = g_Q (order 2) or 2g_Q (else),
  t = Σ t_Q,  w = Σ (u_Q + x_Q t_Q),

  **E′ : y² = x³ + (a − 5t)x + (b − 7w)**,

  **X(x) = x + Σ_Q [ t_Q/(x − x_Q) + u_Q/(x − x_Q)² ]**, φ = (X, y·X′(x)).

Structure worth reading off before ever plugging in numbers:

* X(x) has **poles exactly at the kernel x-coordinates** — kernel points
  are the ones sent "to infinity", i.e. to O′. The map's shape displays
  its kernel;
* X = x + (correction terms): near infinity φ ≈ identity — Vélu's
  choice is **normalized**, φ*(dx′/2y′) = dx/2y, which is what makes the
  slick y-coordinate rule Y = y·X′(x) correct (differentiate the
  invariant differential; lab 05 implements exactly this and verifies
  the result lands on E′);
* everything is symmetric functions of the kernel — rational in the
  base field whenever the *subgroup* is Galois-stable, even if its
  individual points live upstairs.

**Degree 2, fully explicit** (the workhorse of labs 05–06): kernel
{O, (x₀, 0)}: t = 3x₀² + a, w = x₀t,

  E′: y² = x³ + (a − 5t)x + (b − 7w),
  φ(x, y) = ( x + t/(x − x₀), y·(1 − t/(x − x₀)²) ).

*Worked micro-example* (check by hand): E: y² = x³ + x over F₂₃,
x₀ = 0: t = a = 1, w = 0. E′: y² = x³ − 4x, φ(x,y) = (x + 1/x,
y(1 − 1/x²)). Take P = (1, 5) (1 + 1 = 2 ≡ 5² = 25 ✓): φ(P) = (2, 0).
On E′: 8 − 8 = 0 ✓ — so φ(P) is 2-torsion, which forces
2P ∈ ker φ; indeed the doubling formula gives 2P = (0, 0), the kernel
generator (check it: λ = 4/10 ≡ 5, x₃ = 25 − 2 ≡ 0, y₃ = 0). Lab 05
does this 2 000 more times with random points so you don't have to.

## 5. The dual isogeny

**Theorem.** Every isogeny φ : E → E′ of degree m has a unique **dual**
φ̂ : E′ → E with

  **φ̂ ∘ φ = [m] on E**, φ ∘ φ̂ = [m] on E′,

and deg φ̂ = m, φ̂̂ = φ, dual of a composite = composite of duals
(reversed).

Consequences:

* isogeny is symmetric: E ∼ E′ ⟺ E′ ∼ E — so isogeny *graphs* can be
  drawn undirected (away from the automorphism quirks at j = 0, 1728);
* φ̂∘φ = [m] is the identity you *computed* in lab 05 — where the check
  taught the practical lesson that Vélu returns to an isomorphic model,
  not the identical one, and the u² bookkeeping of module 08 §1 is
  needed to compare;
* **isogenous curves over F_q have the same number of points** (Tate's
  isogeny theorem — same Frobenius trace). Lab 05 checks this equality
  for every isogeny it builds; lab 06 relied on it (all curves in one
  supersingular graph share #E = (p ∓ 1)²).

## 6. Modular polynomials: isogeny without points

For each ℓ there is a symmetric polynomial Φ_ℓ(X, Y) ∈ ℤ[X, Y] with:

  **Φ_ℓ(j(E), j(E′)) = 0 ⟺ E and E′ are ℓ-isogenous.**

Adjacency in the ℓ-isogeny graph is a *polynomial identity in
j-invariants* — no curves, no kernels, no points. Φ₂ is already bulky:

  Φ₂ = X³ + Y³ − X²Y² + 1488(X²Y + XY²) − 162000(X² + Y²)
     + 40773375XY + 8748000000(X + Y) − 157464000000000,

(Φ₃ has 60-digit coefficients; nobody writes Φ_ℓ by hand). Given j,
the ℓ + 1 roots of Φ_ℓ(j, Y) are exactly the neighbours — an
alternative to Vélu for walking graphs, and the tool inside SEA point
counting (module 07 §3). Lab 06 plays it as referee: every Vélu-built
edge is confirmed with Φ₂(j₁, j₂) = 0. Two independent mathematical
routes, one answer — the best kind of test.

## 7. Self-check

<details><summary><b>Q1.</b> [m] has degree m² but the "size" of its
formulas (division polynomials) is ~m²/2 in x. Reconcile deg [m] = m²
with #E[m] = m² and with separability.</summary>

deg [m] = m² and [m] is separable (for p ∤ m), so #ker = deg = m²:
consistent with E[m] ≅ (ℤ/m)². The x-coordinate map has degree m² too,
with x-degree numerator m² — but x-coordinates identify P and −P, so
ψ_m² vanishing on (m²−1)/2 x-values suffices. No contradiction, just
the 2-to-1 x-projection.
</details>

<details><summary><b>Q2.</b> Why does a cyclic kernel of order ℓ (prime)
over F_{p²} force x_Q-symmetric functions into F_{p²} even when the
points (x_Q, y_Q) live in a bigger field?</summary>

The subgroup is stable under Galois (it's the kernel of an isogeny
defined over F_{p²}), so Galois permutes the kernel points among
themselves and fixes every symmetric function of them. Vélu's t and w
are such symmetric functions — the formulas stay downstairs. This is
Galois theory earning its keep in a computer program.
</details>

<details><summary><b>Q3.</b> Compose φ (degree 2) with its dual and
take degrees: what identity on deg do you get, and what does it say
about the "size" of [2]?</summary>

deg(φ̂∘φ) = 2·2 = 4 = deg [2] = 2². Multiplication by 2 *is* a
degree-4 map — as the x₃ = λ² − 2x₁ doubling formula's degree-4
rational function confirms directly.
</details>

<details><summary><b>Q4.</b> From Φ₂ being degree 3 in each variable,
re-derive "every vertex of the 2-isogeny graph has 3 outgoing edges",
and connect to E[2].</summary>

Roots of Φ₂(j, Y): ≤ 3 neighbours, with equality (counting
multiplicity) since Φ₂(j, Y) is degree exactly 3 (leading coefficient
issues only at special j). Structurally: 3 = ℓ + 1 = number of order-2
subgroups of E[2] ≅ (ℤ/2)² = lines in an F₂-plane (module 07 §2). Two
proofs, one number — and lab 06 asserted `len(nbrs) == 3` for every
vertex it ever built.
</details>

## 8. Where this goes

You can now *build* any prime-degree isogeny from its kernel and
*recognize* one from j-invariants alone. Module 10 turns the crank:
all supersingular j's + all 2-isogenies = one graph, and its shocking
property — optimal expansion — is what cryptography spends.
