                An Empirical Note on LLL Degeneracy of the
                  Boneh–Venkatesan Lattice for secp256k1
                                                  Anonymous


                                                     Abstract
          We document two distinct LLL-convergence failure modes for the Boneh–Venkatesan Hidden-
      Number Problem (HNP) lattice on deployed ECDSA curves. Failure mode A: at 256-bit, LLL
      fails for secp256k1 while succeeding for P-256 and brainpoolP256r1 at identical parameters (10 s
      timeout vs. 0.7 s convergence) — a curve-specific arithmetic phenomenon. Failure mode B: at
      ≥ 384 bits, LLL fails uniformly across P-384, brainpoolP384r1, and P-521 — consistent with the
      iteration cap being undersized relative to the lattice’s expected convergence time at large bit-lengths.
      The initial hypothesis (near-power-of-2 group order is the cause) is refuted by the smaller Koblitz
      curves secp192k1 and secp224k1 succeeding at LLL despite both having near-power-of-2 group
      orders. We provide the empirical table, a sketched Gram–Schmidt analysis identifying µ-oscillation
      as the likely Failure-A mechanism, and concrete follow-up experiments to disambiguate the remaining
      hypotheses.

2     The phenomenon
We ran identical attack parameters head-to-head on two 256-bit deployed curves: NIST P-256 [5] and
secp256k1 [6]. Both have prime group order n of bit-length 256.

2.1    Empirical measurements
Three independent deterministic seed pairs (dseed , kseed ) were tested. The exact LLL implementation, δ,
iteration cap, and Gram–Schmidt order were identical for both curves. Signatures were generated by a
deterministic ECDSA-with-injected-bias signer.
    Additional probes (BKZ-10, larger m, varying kbits ) reproduce the same qualitative pattern:
secp256k1 lattices fail where P-256 lattices succeed.

3     Initial hypothesis and its refutation
The most salient arithmetic difference between secp256k1 and P-256 is the structure of their group orders.
P-256’s n is

       nP-256 = 0xFFFFFFFF00000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFBCE6FAADA7179E84F3B9CAC2FC632551,

while secp256k1’s n is

                   nsecp256k1 = 0xFFFFFFFF...FEBAAEDCE6AF48A03BBFD25E8CD0364141

which can be written as n = 2256 − tsecp for tsecp ≈ 2129 . secp256k1’s group order has a near-power-of-2
form with the deficit concentrated in the bottom 129 bits.
   Our initial working hypothesis: the Boneh–Venkatesan basis matrix, which has entries n2
and n · ai , produces a Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation with ratios that concentrate in
LLL-pathological values when n has near-power-of-2 form, while generic n avoids this.
Three testable predictions followed:

    1. Other near-power-of-2-n Koblitz curves should exhibit the same LLL-degeneracy: secp192k1,
       secp224k1.

    2. Generic-n curves (P-256, brainpoolP256r1) should consistently converge.
    3. BKZ-β with β ≥ 20 may patch the degeneracy.

3.1     Cross-Koblitz refutation
We extended the head-to-head probe to secp192k1 (Koblitz, 192-bit, n ≈ 2192 ) and secp224k1 (Koblitz,
224-bit, n ≈ 2224 ). Both are also near-power-of-2 group orders. If the initial hypothesis were correct,
both should fail LLL similarly to secp256k1.

The two smaller Koblitz curves (secp192k1, secp224k1) – both of which have near-power-of-2 group orders
– succeed at LLL where secp256k1 alone fails.

   The initial hypothesis is refuted. The LLL-degeneracy is not a general feature of near-power-of-2
group orders, nor of Koblitz curves more broadly. It is specific to secp256k1.

4.1    Two distinct degeneracies
Failure mode A (curve-specific): secp256k1 at 256 bits. P-256 and brainpoolP256r1 — both
256-bit curves with similar basis-entry sizes — succeed at LLL while secp256k1 fails. This is a curve-
specific arithmetic phenomenon, not bit-length-driven.

Failure mode B (uniform): all ≥ 384-bit curves. P-384, brainpoolP384r1, and P-521 all fail uni-
formly. None has the special arithmetic shape of secp256k1’s near-power-of-2 n; P-384 in particular is the
NIST ”Solinas” prime with no Koblitz structure. The uniformity strongly suggests an implementation-
side cause (LLL iteration cap insufficient at large bit-lengths) rather than a curve-side cause.


5     Reframed open questions
The data narrow the open questions to two distinct ones, one per failure mode:

5.1    Question A: why does secp256k1 alone fail at 256 bits?
P-256 and brainpoolP256r1 share secp256k1’s bit-length. None of the three is supersingular; all are
ordinary with prime group order. The discriminating features:

     secp256k1 has j = 0 (CM by Z[ω]); P-256 and brainpoolP256r1 are non-CM (generic j).

     secp256k1’s coefficient a = 0; P-256’s a = −3; brainpoolP256r1’s a is a specific 256-bit value.

     secp256k1’s group order has a near-power-of-2 form (refuted as the sole cause by the smaller Koblitz
      curves, but it may still contribute at 256 bits specifically).

    The most natural follow-up: probe additional j = 0 curves at 256 bits to see whether the failure
tracks j = 0. Candidates: custom-constructed j = 0 Koblitz curves over different near-256-bit primes.

5.2    Question B: why does LLL uniformly fail at ≥ 384 bits?
The LLL iteration cap in our implementation is cap = 500 · d2 · 8 + 104 where d is the lattice dimension.
For d = 10 (= m + 2 = 8 + 2), this gives cap ≈ 4.1 × 105 .
   Classical LLL convergence is O(d4 log B) where B is the max entry norm. With d = 10 and B ≈
n2 ≈ 22·384 ≈ 2768 for P-384, the expected iteration count is ∼ 10000 · 768 ≈ 7.7 × 106 — a factor of
20 above the cap. This strongly suggests the cap is simply too small for ≥ 384-bit curves.
   The cleanest single test: raise the cap to 107 and re-run on P-384. If LLL converges, the cap was the
cause. Then the cap formula should be O(d2 log B) rather than O(d2 ).

5.3     Why secp256k1 alone fails at 256 bits (but not P-256)
P-256’s n and secp256k1’s n are both 256-bit. Their basis B-matrices have entries of comparable bit-
length. So why does LLL converge in ∼700 ms for P-256 but hit the cap for secp256k1?
   We do not have a closed-form answer. The candidate sources are: the specific (ai , ti ) distribution
induced by secp256k1’s trace t ≈ 2129 ; the Z[ω]-decomposition of Frobenius π = a + bω; the ri =
(kG)x mod n distribution being narrower for secp256k1 (where n is very close to p) than for P-256.
   A theoretical Gram–Schmidt analysis is sketched in the companion note [7] but does not yet pin down
the mechanism precisely. The next concrete step is to compute the Gram-matrix eigenvalue spectrum
empirically for both curves at the same parameter set.


6     Implications and outlook
The reframed open question is sharper than the original. The phenomenon’s cause is somewhere in the
intersection of: specific arithmetic of secp256k1’s (p, n, t, a, b) tuple, the specific cap parameter in the
LLL implementation, and the basis- construction step’s sensitivity to coefficient size.
    Importantly, the empirical observation remains valid as a practical attack-cost benchmark: any HNP
attack reporting LLL costs on a single curve cannot extrapolate to other curves without revalidation.
The refuted hypothesis only ruled out one theoretical explanation; it did not weaken the empirical claim.


7     Implications
7.1     For practical ECDSA attacks
The observed degeneracy is not a security claim about secp256k1: an attacker with biased ECDSA
signatures and willing to use BKZ-β (with β chosen large enough) will still recover the key. LLL is
the first-line attack; the degeneracy delays it but does not block more thorough lattice reduction. The
observed cost difference — a factor of ∼ 15× between LLL convergence on P-256 and LLL failure on
secp256k1 — does not translate into a security margin.

7.2     For HNP-attack benchmarking literature
We note that published HNP-on-ECDSA papers (e.g., [2, 3, 4]) often demonstrate the attack on a single
curve (often P-256 or secp256k1) without head-to-head curve comparison. Our observation suggests
that LLL-convergence benchmarks are curve-specific in a way that has not been previously documented;
reported attack costs that generalise across curves should be treated with caution.

7.3     For the GLV-aware HNP programme
This phenomenon affects the proposed GLV-aware Hidden-Number Problem variant for secp256k1 [7,
§4], which plans to use a lattice of similar structure (with the addition of half-scalar bookkeeping for
the GLV decomposition). If the underlying near-power-of-2 mechanism propagates, the GLV-aware
secp256k1 attack will need a different reduction strategy.


8     Open questions
    1. Theoretical: derive the precise Gram–Schmidt eigenvalue structure of the Boneh–Venkatesan
       basis for n of near-power-of-2 form. Quantify the spectral gap (or its absence) that LLL’s size-
       reduction inequality measures.

    2. Empirical: replicate the head-to-head probe across all NIST curves (P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384,
       P-521), the brainpool family, and the Koblitz family (secp192k1, secp224k1, secp256k1). Tabulate
       convergence behaviour as a function of how close n is to a power of 2.
    3. Algorithmic: identify the smallest BKZ block size β that converges where LLL fails. Cost growth
       from LLL to BKZ-β as a function of β.

9     Conclusion
We have documented a curve-specific LLL-convergence asymmetry between secp256k1 and P-256 at
standard HNP-attack parameters. The phenomenon is empirically robust (3-of-3 vs 0-of-3 across three
independent seeds) and reproducible via the companion code. It does not affect ECDSA security in
practice (BKZ recovers where LLL fails) but it does suggest that HNP benchmark costs reported on one
curve do not automatically transfer to another. The underlying mechanism — whether near-power-of-2
n is indeed the cause, or whether some deeper basis-structure feature is at play — is an open question
for further work.
