1 Introduction
Semaev's summation polynomials [7] translate the statement  m points of an elliptic curve
sum to the identity into a polynomial condition on their x-coordinates alone, and they are
the algebraic engine of index-calculus attacks on the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem
(ECDLP) [5, 1, 2]. A recurring hope in that line of work is that some choice  of curve, of
curve family, of factor base  might raise the rate at which relations are found. This paper
makes three statements about which choices can possibly matter, and nds that a specic,
identiable class of them cannot.
   The main object is the cover of parameter space cut out by Sm in its last variable. Write
n = m − 1, let t1 , . . . , tn be independent transcendentals over K , and consider

What this paper does not claim

It claims no improvement to any ECDLP algorithm, no security reduction, no new hardness
assumption, and no change to any complexity estimate. Every asymptotic statement about
index calculus in the literature stands untouched. The results are structural facts about a
family of polynomials, plus one combinatorial identity, and their content for cryptanalysis is
exclusively that of ruling certain proposals out.
      It also claims no novelty for its classical ingredients. The identication of the roots as
signed sums, the degree 2

What is oered as possibly new is the Galois-theoretic identication for all m (Theorem 3.4),
the exact factorization law (Theorem 4.2), and the synthesis in 7. 9 states honestly what
search was and was not possible from the environment in which this work was done, and does

5.1    Why complete splitting is not an algorithm

A reader meeting Theorem 5.1 for the rst time may suspect it trivializes relation search. It
does not, and the reason is an asymmetry between which variables are given and which are
sought.
   Theorem 5.1 is a statement about solving for the last coordinate given the rst m − 1.
Its operational content is: choose P1 , . . . , Pm−1 ∈ F ; then the 2
                                                                        m−2 admissible values of the

remaining coordinate are x(±P1 ± · · · ± Pm−1 ), computable by O(2
                                                                             m−2 ) group operations.

That is precisely the solve for the last variable and test membership step that index calculus
already performs, and it is why the summation-polynomial system is usually posed in m − 1
rather than m unknowns.
      Relation search asks the opposite question. The target x(R) is xed, and one must nd
a1 , . . . , am−1 in the factor base with Sm (a1 , . . . , am−1 , x(R)) = 0. Complete splitting in the
last variable says nothing about the existence or the cost of nding such a tuple: it does not
make the factor base membership test cheaper, it does not change how many tuples must be
tried, and it does not change the algebraic diculty of the system when one does pose it as
a polynomial system. The cost of index calculus sits in that search and in the subsequent
linear algebra, neither of which Theorem 5.1 touches.
   What Theorem 5.1 does give is negative and methodological, and it is the reason the
theorem is stated at all:

cycle type, or Frobenius class of the bre of Sm  is identically constant on the factor-base
locus, at every m and for every curve. In particular, a Chebotarev-style census of generic
