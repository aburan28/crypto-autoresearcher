                                                  Abstract
        Index calculus is the method that should exploit the most structure in an elliptic-curve discrete-
    logarithm instance, and in practice it has never beaten Pollard rho on a prime-order curve of crypto-
    graphic shape. This paper surveys where that question stands and measures it. We review the liter-
    ature by eld typeextension elds (Gaudry, Diem, FGHR), prime-degree binary elds and the con-
    tested rst-fall-degree heuristics behind the subexponential claims (PetitQuisquater, Semaev, and
    the evidence of Huang, Kosters and Yeo, ShantzTeske and GalbraithGebregiyorgis), subeld curves
    (GalbraithGrangerMerzPetit) and prime elds (PetitKostersMesseng, AmadoriPintoreSala,
    McGuireMueller)and the generic lower bounds that frame them.
        We then measure the frontier with one native harness that charges rho, baby-step giant-step, the
    kangaroo and several index-calculus pipelines in a single counted unit on one-target workloads with
    sealed, replayable records. Almost no cost in such records is complete, the rho baselines included,
    so we price every arm's unpriced native work alike across a band of weights and rank two methods
    only where the order holds across the band. On xed ladders of eight Koblitz curves (log2 r from 11
    to 39), six NIST-shaped prime curves (2040 bits) and ve Kummer-tower curves4,848 executions
    on public targets, parameters chosen on separate training targets, every session replay-auditedno
    index-calculus arm beats generic rho on any curve at any weight. The closest, a compact Frobenius-
    orbit factor base, is 3.815× signed-Frobenius rho with native work free; two-summand prime-eld
    index calculus goes from 10.5× to 102× rho between 20 and 40 bits; no tted cost exponent of a
    campaign index-calculus arm lies below rho's.
        The measurements come from a fourteen-week engineering programme that we also document:
    root-nding decomposition oracles (a known mechanism; its root identity, Proposition 1, is proved
    in Lean, and the oracle is engineered to 3831,535× over matrix F4 per decomposition), an Fp F4
    brought to msolve parity on square systems, FGHR's D4 symmetry cutting the k = 4 solve over
    Fp4 by 48×, genus-three index calculus after the cubic GHS transfer at about q 1.4 against rho's
    q 3/2 (the only exponent below rho's; its distance to a measured negation-map rho at q = 220 fell
    from 2.6× to 1.15× in three improvement rounds), and the negative results, including a conservation
    lemma for isogeny transfers and an equal-advice audit that retires the programme's online win over
    rho: BernsteinLange preprocessing rho with the same table is 53317× faster. We close with open
    problems, each stated as the experiment or proof that would settle it.      Nothing here concerns a
    deployed curve or key recovery.

1 Introduction
For a prime-order subgroup ⟨G⟩ of size r on an elliptic curve E/Fq , the best generic algorithm for the

Index calculus on elliptic curves.       Fix a factor base F ⊂ E(Fq ). A relation is a decomposition
aG + bQ = R = P1 + · · · + Pm with Pi ∈ F ; once about |F| relations are found, linear algebra over Z/r

algebra, verication) and a sixth, the comparison with rho on the same target. The decomposition oracle
decides whether R splits over F .     Semaev [21] reduced it to the roots of the summation polynomial
Sm+1 (x1 , . . . , xm , xR ), which vanishes exactly when ±P1 ± · · · ± Pm = R for points with those abscissae.
Over F2n or Fpk the factor base is chosen as the points whose abscissa lies in a subspace or subeld,
and Weil restriction turns Sm+1 = 0 into a polynomial system over the base eld; the textbook oracle
solves that system with a Gröbner-basis engine (F4/F5, [5, 6]) or a SAT solver [24]. Gaudry [12] and
Diem [4] give the asymptotics over extension elds; for prime elds no factor base is known that makes the
method competitive, and summation-polynomial systems over Fp are the subject of the Kummer-tower
and rational-map bases of PetitKostersMesseng [19].

3 The theory, by eld type
3.1    What a decomposition costs
Every index-calculus algorithm for elliptic curves in the literature spends its time in three places: nding
about |F| relations (each a decomposition of a random combination aG + bQ over F ), solving the sparse
linear system over Z/r , and, in some variants, a descent of the target. The second and third are well
understood. The rst is the crux: a uniformly random point decomposes over a base of size N into m
                                       m
summands with probability about N          /(m! r), so relations need about m! r/N m−1 trials, and each trial
costs whatever the decomposition oracle costs.         Every result below is a statement about one of three
things: how large N can be while membership stays algebraically testable, how cheap the oracle is, and
how the two trade against the linear algebra.



3.2    Extension elds Fqk with k > 1 xed or small
Gaudry [12] takes F to be the points with abscissa in the base eld Fq and decomposes into m = k
summands by Weil restriction of Sk+1 , a system of k equations in k unknowns over Fq . For xed k this

gives Õ(q     ), asymptotically below rho's q k/2 for k ≥ 3; the constant hidden in the Gröbner solve
grows very quickly with k , which is what makes the crossover large in practice (to be quantied from the
paper). Diem [4] extends the analysis to families where k grows, proving subexponential complexity over
sequences of elds with suitably balanced q and k . Joux and Vitse's work on decomposition and cover

of the 2-torsion to replace Sm+1 by its invariant form, cutting the Gröbner work by large factors; our
measurement at k = 4 (9.4) is a direct instance.

3.3    Binary elds F2n with n prime
Prime n leaves no subeld, so the factor base is taken to be the points whose abscissa lies in an F2 -vector
subspace V of dimension ℓ. Weil restriction then gives a quadratic-to-low-degree Boolean system whose
diculty is governed by its degree of regularity. The whole debate about binary curves is a debate about
that degree.


The subexponential claims.           Petit and Quisquater [18] conjectured, from experiments and heuristic
evidence, that these systems have degrees of regularity only slightly above the degrees of their equations

(the rst fall degree assumption ), and deduced a heuristic 2                    , c < 2, for a variant of Diem's
algorithm, beating generic methods only for n ≳ 2000. Semaev [22] split summation polynomials into
cubic Boolean systems, argued for regularity degree at most 4 under the same kind of assumption, and
                       √
                      c n ln n
obtained a heuristic 2           with c ≈ 1.69; its rst version concluded that several FIPS binary curves
fall faster than Pollard rho, a conclusion its revision says does not hold under the weaker assumption

The evidence against.       Shantz and Teske [23] tested the PetitQuisquater heuristics experimentally.
Huang, Kosters and Yeo [15] introduced the last fall degree, independent of the monomial order, gave
computational evidence against the rst fall degree assumption in characteristic 2, and constructed
summation-polynomial Weil descent systems where it is unlikely to hold.               Galbraith and Gebregiyor-
gis [9] improved the variable choice (binary Edwards curves), found symmetry-breaking factor bases and
SAT solvers inconclusive, and concluded that rho remains much faster for prime-degree binary elds of
reasonable size. The GalbraithGaudry survey [8] reaches the same verdict for characteristic-two curves
over prime-degree elds.


Where it stands.      No proof or refutation of a subexponential algorithm for prime-degree binary curves
exists. The open quantity is the solving degree of Weil-descended Sm+1 as n and m grow; recent work
supplies diagnostics (saturation-exponent bounds, multi-graded regularity, tests for where systems stop
looking generic) but no bound for these systems. Our own registered refutation-degree law at m = 3
(7) is one data point and ended inconclusive.

3.5    Prime elds
Over Fp there is no subeld and no subspace, and no factor base is known for which membership is both
dense enough and algebraically testable. Semaev's original small-abscissa base leads to systems of very
high degree. Petit, Kosters and Messeng [19] construct bases from rational maps (including the Kummer
tower µ2t ) that give the summation system extra structure; Amadori, Pintore and Sala [1] build the base
from the instance and need only one relation search, improving on both; McGuire and Mueller [17] avoid
Gröbner bases altogether by fast evaluation of Sm through iterated resultants, and in a second variant
by summing m − 1 factor-base points and testing membership of the resultthe same mechanism as
the two-summand root-nding oracle of 6. All of these remain exponential and slower than rho; a 2024
analysis again concludes that no subexponential prime-eld method is known.

6 Root-nding decomposition
6.1    Origin: a suggestion in an audited manuscript
The textbook decomposition oracle builds Semaev's polynomial system and solves it with a Gröbner-basis
engine. On 12 October a 13-page manuscript, Point Decomposition by Root Finding, was supplied for
assessment. We froze it (research/ecbench_rootfinding_assessment_20261012/source-paper.pdf,
SHA-256 743d3d3e...7c01) and audited it against its source branch before measuring anything.            Its
central suggestion is simple: for two summands, xing one factor-base point turns S3 into a quadratic in
the remaining variable whose roots are already known.


Proposition 1 (roots of S3 ). Let E : y 2 = x3 + ax + b over a eld K of characteristic ̸= 2, 3, and let
R, F ∈ E(K) be ane with xR ̸= xF . Then

                 S3 (xR , xF , X) = (xR − xF )2 (X − x+ )(X − x− ),     x± = x(R ± F ),

so S3 (xR , xF , X) = 0 i X ∈ {x(R + F ), x(R − F )}.

Proof sketch. With λ± = (±yF − yR )/(xF − xR ) the chord slopes, x± = λ± − xR − xF . Expanding
(X − x+ )(X − x− ) and eliminating yR , yF with the curve equation gives exactly Semaev's S3 /(xR − xF )2 .

The identity is machine-checked over an arbitrary eld in Lean 4 with Mathlib (papers/rootfinding-d
ecomposition/lean/S3Roots.lean, theorems SemaevS3.S3_factor and SemaevS3.S3_root_iff; no
sorry; axioms propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound).
   Hence the two-summand system has a parametrised variety: R = ±Fi ± Fj i x(R ∓ Fi ) ∈ x(F). No
polynomial system is solved; the oracle is one subtraction and one table probe per base point.


Prior art.   Neither the identity nor the oracle is new. The identity is a direct consequence of how S3 is
dened: it is the polynomial whose roots are the abscissae of P1 ± P2 . The oracle mechanismsum m − 1
factor-base points in the group and test whether the result lies in the baseis the second algorithm of
McGuire and Mueller [17], who also evaluate Sm by iterated resultants instead of solving it, building on
the instance-derived bases of Amadori, Pintore and Sala [1]. What this section adds is engineering and
measurement: a machine-checked proof, a fail-closed verication protocol, binary pair-sum tables for up
to four summands, GPU, Kummer-tower and dense-index variants, and complete-cost measurements in
one harness against rho.


What the audit kept and corrected.         The identity is exact under the stated generic ane condition,
and a returned witness can be re-added in the group for soundness.       Completeness of the exhaustive
enumeration holds only with an explicit sign and factor-base convention (the binary table stores full
point keys over a base containing both signs; the manuscript's schematic        x-only table needed that
convention stated). The manuscript's NIST baseline was Buchberger, not F4 (4.5), and its statement
that no implementation can use fewer than linear eld operations per ideal fails for repeated queries
against a reusable pair table.   Larger-summand enumeration has a growing exponent in |F|, and the
manuscript did not benchmark it end to end. All of this is recorded in research/ecbench_rootfindi
ng_assessment_20261012/REPORT.md.
